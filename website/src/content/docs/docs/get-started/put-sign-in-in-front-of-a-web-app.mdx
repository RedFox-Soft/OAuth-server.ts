Some web apps have no sign-in of their own: a dashboard, an internal tool, an admin page someone wrote in an
afternoon. Anyone who finds the address can open them. You can fix that without touching the app. Put it behind your
proxy, and let the proxy ask FoxAuth about every request before the request reaches the app.

This way of working is called **forward auth**. Your reverse proxy (Caddy, Traefik or nginx) stands in front of the
app. For each request, it asks your FoxAuth project whether the request may go through. A person who has not signed
in is sent to the project's sign-in page. A person who may open the page is let through, and the app is told who
they are. A person who may not open it sees a page that says so.

This page takes about ten minutes if your proxy already serves the app.

<Aside
	type="note"
	title="What you need first"
>
	A running instance with an administrator account ([Create the first
	administrator](/docs/get-started/first-admin/)), a project with people in it,
	and at least one group of those people. The app must be served over `https` by
	a proxy you can configure.
</Aside>

## 1. Create the app in the console {#create-the-app}

1. Open your project and choose its **Applications** tab, then **New application**.
2. Choose **A web app with no sign-in of its own**.
3. Fill in four things:
   - **Name**: how the console names the app.
   - **Address**: the app's own `https` address, such as `https://grafana.example.com`. Only the scheme, the host and
     a port are kept. If you paste an address with a path or a query, the form drops them and says so.
   - **Who can open it**: one or more groups. Only their members may open the app at all. You can open parts of it to
     other people later.
   - **Proxy**: Caddy, Traefik or nginx. This only chooses which configuration the next step shows first.

The app appears in its own table on the Applications tab. Its page has three parts: **Connect**, **Access** and
**Settings**.

## 2. Configure your proxy {#configure-your-proxy}

**Connect** shows the configuration for your proxy, with your project's address and your app's id filled in. Copy
it from there. The examples below are the same text, built by the same code, for an app at
`https://grafana.example.com` in a project at `https://auth.example.com/acme`. Your app's id takes the place of
`<appId>`.

Each configuration does three things:

- It sends every request to the check at `https://auth.example.com/acme/verify/<appId>` first, with the request's
  original method, host and path. The check refuses a request that does not say all three.
- It removes any `X-Auth-*` header the browser sent, so nobody can pretend to be someone else.
- It passes the request to the app, with the headers the check answered.

The app itself is assumed to listen on `127.0.0.1:8080`. Replace that with its real address.

## 3. Keep the app reachable only through your proxy {#reachable-only-through-the-proxy}

The check protects only the requests that pass through your proxy. A request that reaches the app another way skips
it, and the app would trust any `X-Auth-*` header it carried.

So make the app listen only where the proxy can reach it: on `127.0.0.1`, on a private network, or behind a firewall
rule that admits only the proxy. Connect repeats this, because it is the one part FoxAuth cannot do for you.

If the app can check a signature, it can also stop depending on this. See `X-Auth-Assertion` below.

## 4. What your app receives {#what-your-app-receives}

When a request is let through, the proxy adds these headers:

| Header             | What it holds                                                                                                                                                                       |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `X-Auth-User`      | The person's identifier in the project. It never changes, so use it as the key for the person.                                                                                      |
| `X-Auth-Email`     | The person's email address.                                                                                                                                                         |
| `X-Auth-Groups`    | The identifiers of the person's groups, separated by commas. Match on these, not on group names: a name can change.                                                                 |
| `X-Auth-Assertion` | The same facts, signed with the project's key. It is a JWT whose audience is your app's address and which is valid for 60 seconds. It also carries the group names.                 |
| `X-Auth-Reason`    | A short code for the decision, such as `allowed`. Refusals carry one too, for example `not_in_group` or `no_session`, so your proxy's access log shows why someone was turned away. |

On a path that is open to anyone, the first four headers are present but empty.

An app that verifies `X-Auth-Assertion` against the keys the project publishes at its `jwks_uri` knows the facts came
from the project. It no longer has to trust that every request came through the proxy.

## 5. Check that it works {#check-that-it-works}

Connect shows four steps, each with the time it first happened:

1. The app was created.
2. The proxy asked this project about the app.
3. Someone signed in through the proxy.
4. Someone got through.

Open the app in a private browser window. You should land on the project's sign-in page, and after signing in, back
on the page you asked for. If step 2 stays empty, the proxy is not sending the check. If Connect names a host that is
not your app's address, the proxy is not passing the original host.

To sign out of the app, open `/_foxauth/sign_out` on the app's own address.

## Choose who opens which part {#access-rules}

**Access** lists one rule per path, as a tree. A rule says who may open its path and everything under it:

- the members of one or more groups;
- everyone the project lets sign in;
- anyone, without signing in (only for `GET` and `HEAD`, and only for that exact path unless you also open
  everything under it);
- no one.

The longest path that matches decides, so the order of the rules never matters. A rule for `/admin` covers
`/admin/users` too, and `/Admin/users` as well. Before a save that lets more people in anywhere, the console lists
what widens and asks you to confirm. The access check on the same tab tells you whether a person would get in at a
given address, now and after you save.
