A passkey signs a person in with their fingerprint, face or screen lock instead of a password. It stays on their
phone, their laptop or in their password manager, and it works only at your project's own address, so a fake sign-in
page cannot use it. This page shows how to turn passkeys on in a project and what your people then see.

## Turn it on {#on}

1. Open the project's **Sign-in** tab. Under **Ways to sign in**, switch on **Passkeys** and save.
2. Check the notes beside the switch:
   - The switch is disabled when the server is served at an IP address. Passkeys need a domain name.
   - Over plain `http`, browsers do not offer passkeys. The console warns about it. `localhost` is the one exception,
     for trying it on your own machine.
   - Adding a passkey needs email, so the console says so when no mail server is set up.

Passkeys are off in every project until you turn them on. The setting is free on every plan. The administrators'
project has no such switch: administrators have no account page to add a passkey on.

An agent turns it on with `project_update` (`passkeyLogin`). Nobody can add a passkey for someone else: only the
person can.

## What a person does {#steps}

1. They sign in and open their account page, **Sign-in and security**, at `/account/security` under your project's
   address. It has a **Passkeys** part above **Two-step verification**.
2. They choose **Add a passkey**. Their browser or phone asks for a fingerprint, face or screen lock and makes the
   passkey.
3. The passkey is listed with a name such as "Chrome on Windows", the date it was created, when it was last used,
   whether it is synced to their devices, and how it was added. FoxAuth emails them that a passkey was added.

Adding a passkey needs JavaScript in the browser. A person can hold up to 20 passkeys in a project.

## Why adding one needs a strong sign-in {#proof}

Someone who learned the password must not be able to add their own passkey. So adding one needs either:

- a sign-in in the last 15 minutes with a passkey, or with the password and a code from an authenticator app; or
- a sign-in with the password in the last 15 minutes, and a link mailed to the account's address, opened in the same
  browser within 10 minutes. A link opened in another browser does nothing.

A person whose address is not confirmed yet, or who signs in only through a provider such as Google, cannot add a
passkey yet. The page says why.

## Signing in {#sign-in}

The sign-in page offers **Sign in with a passkey**. When the person clicks into the email field, the browser also
offers their passkeys for the project in its autofill list. The passkey block is there on every form of the page: with
the password form, on a page with only providers, and on a page that refused a password from a known guessing address.

A passkey sign-in asks for no code, even in a project that requires a code from everyone, and even when the person has
an authenticator app. It counts as two-step sign-in, because the device checks the person and holds the key. So it
meets every requirement of two-step sign-in:

- an app with **Require two-step sign-in**, see [Require two-step sign-in in one app](/docs/get-started/require-a-code-for-an-app/);
- an API with **Require two-step sign-in** on its **Access** tab;
- a path behind your proxy with **with two-step sign-in** on its **Ask to sign in again** condition;
- an app that asks for the multi-factor `acr` value itself.

The page that tells a person an app needs two-step sign-in also offers **Sign in with a passkey** in a project that
accepts passkeys.

When a passkey cannot be used, the page says only "This passkey isn't for _project_. Choose another one, or sign in
another way." It says the same for a passkey of another project, an unknown one, and one that may have been copied.
After 30 such failures in an hour from one network, passkey sign-in is refused there until the hour ends. A locked or
deactivated account gets "This account can't sign in right now." with no word about a password.

## The offer after a password sign-in {#offer}

After someone signs in with their password, FoxAuth asks whether to create a passkey before it sends them back to
your app. The page explains what a passkey is and says that the password keeps working. It has **Create a passkey**
and **Not now**, and from the second time also **Don't ask again**.

- After a password **and a code** from an authenticator app, the new passkey counts as two-step sign-in at once.
- After a password **alone**, the new passkey works like the password until the person confirms it. FoxAuth emails
  them a **Yes, this was me** link, which works only in the browser where they made the passkey, within 72 hours.
  Until then, a sign-in with it is a password sign-in: an app that requires two-step sign-in still asks for a code, and
  a password reset removes the passkey. Someone who only knows the password gains nothing lasting from it.

The offer shows at most once in 30 days for each person, in every app of the project. It is not shown after a sign-in
through a provider or with a passkey, when **Remember me** was unticked, on a device that cannot make a passkey, or
after another page in the same sign-in, such as setting up an authenticator app or consent.

Turn it off with **Offer a passkey after a password sign-in**, under **Passkeys** on the Sign-in tab (an agent uses
`project_update` with `passkeyOffer`). People can still add passkeys on their own page.

## Rename and remove {#manage}

On **Sign-in and security** a person can rename a passkey after a sign-in in the last 15 minutes. A name has 1 to 64
characters, on one line.

Removing one needs the same proof as adding one. It stops signing them in at once, and FoxAuth emails them that it was
removed. The last way into the project cannot be removed: a person with no password the project accepts and no
provider must add another way first.

A passkey can be marked:

- **Made for an old address**: the project's address changed since it was made, so it no longer works;
- **Refused: it may have been copied**: the device reported a use count lower than the last one FoxAuth saw.

Both can be removed like any other.

## If someone else added one {#not-me}

The email about a new passkey has a **This wasn't me** link. Within 72 hours it removes that passkey and every passkey
added after it, and signs the account out everywhere. When the person has a password and the project lets people reset
it, FoxAuth also emails them a link to set a new one, since someone else knows the password. The link works even after
whoever added the passkey signed the account out everywhere.

## What the browser is told {#browser}

On **Sign-in and security**, browsers that support it are told which of the person's passkeys your project still
accepts and their current address, so the password manager stops offering a removed passkey. At a project with its
own subdomain or hostname, a passkey that no project on the server knows is reported unknown after it is refused, so
the password manager can delete it. At the server's shared host this never happens, because that host also holds other
projects' passkeys.

Each host also answers `/.well-known/passkey-endpoints`, which password managers use for their "Manage" link. A
project with its own subdomain or hostname names its **Sign-in and security** page. The server's shared host names no
page, so a password manager never sends someone into another project.

## One address {#address}

A passkey belongs to the address it was made at:

- every project at a path of the server, and the default project, share the server's host, such as
  `auth.example.com`. A passkey of one of them is still refused at another;
- a project at a subdomain, such as `acme.auth.example.com`, or at a hostname of its own, has its own.

So when you move a project between a path, a subdomain and a hostname, or move the server's `ISSUER` to another host,
its people's passkeys stop working. They are listed as made for an old address, and people add new ones. A new path
keeps them working, since every path shares the server's host.

<Aside type="caution">
	A person whose only way in is a passkey cannot sign in after such a move. Make
	sure people also have a password or a provider before you move a project.
</Aside>

## Turning it off {#off}

Switching **Passkeys** off keeps every passkey people have. They are listed with a note that the project does not
accept them now, and they work again when you switch it back on.

If someone could then no longer sign in, because a passkey is their only way in, the console first shows how many
people that is and asks you to confirm. An agent gets the same number to confirm.

## For your app {#app}

Your app receives the same ID token as after a password and a code: `acr` is your server's multi-factor value, and
`amr` is `["pop", "user", "mfa"]`. Only an app that asks for a passkey receives a different `acr`: see below. A passkey made after a password alone and not yet confirmed reports the password
`acr` and `["pop", "user"]`. A sign-in with a passkey shows as "Passkey" in the person's recent sign-ins, in the
email about a sign-in from somewhere new, and in the project's activity.

Add a link named "Sign-in and security" to your app's account settings that opens `/account/security` under your
project's issuer. People who want a code from an authenticator app instead find it on the same page: see
[Let people turn on two-step verification](/docs/get-started/turn-on-two-step-verification/).

## Require a passkey {#require}

A passkey can't be used by a fake sign-in page, so it is the only phishing-resistant sign-in this server has. You can
require a confirmed passkey in three places once the project accepts passkeys:

- **One application.** Turn on **Require a passkey** in the app's Settings. Before it saves, the console shows how many
  people have no confirmed passkey. A person turned away sees a page that lets them create a passkey there, or sign in
  with the one they hold, and go on to the app. People who sign in through a provider, and devices that sign in with a
  code shown on a TV, can't open the app this way.
- **One API or MCP server.** Choose **Passkey** under **Sign-in required** on the API's **Access** tab. Every access
  token for that API (JWT or opaque introspection) carries the phishing-resistant context (`phr` by default), even when
  the client app did not request it, and tokens checked at your proxy get a `401` challenge with `acr_values` when they
  fall short. See [Protect an MCP server at your
  proxy](/docs/get-started/protect-an-mcp-server-at-your-proxy/#require-a-code-or-a-recent-sign-in).
- **One path behind your proxy.** Choose **With a passkey** on **Ask to sign in again** (or on a **Network facts**
  sign-in-again outcome) for a sensitive path such as `/payouts`. See [Add conditions at your
  proxy](/docs/get-started/add-conditions-at-your-proxy/#with-code).

Turning **Passkeys** off on the project, or moving the project to an address that stops its passkeys working, previews
and names every application, API, and proxy path that requires a passkey. Turning passkeys off leaves them closed until
passkeys are back on or the requirement is lowered.

To ask for a passkey only on some pages of your own app, send `acr_values=phr` (the OpenID value for a phishing-resistant sign-in; the
**Connect** tab shows your server's value). The ID token carries `phr` only after a passkey sign-in. Made essential in
the `claims` parameter, it turns people without a passkey away with `unmet_authentication_requirements`. When your app
names values, it is told the strongest of them the sign-in met, so an app that asks for the multi-factor value keeps
receiving it after a passkey.

## Upgrading {#upgrading}

Run `bun run db:setup` (or `bun run db:setup:pg`) after upgrading, so the server has its places to store passkeys and
their indexes, then `bun run db:migrate`. After that, an older release refuses to start against the database.
